What is this?

80% of returns in most asset classes come from macro. Every individual stock and the broad market decompose into market returns, sector returns, and fundamental returns. Market and sector returns are macro-driven; fundamentals stack on top. If you can map the macro regime correctly, you are already capturing 80% of the available return before you even pick a name — that is why the entire framework starts at the macro level and works down.

It begins with growth, the upstream driver. Growth feeds inflation — together they define the regime (reflation, goldilocks, stagflation, deflation) and form what the central bank is reacting to. That reaction shows up in yields, the operating system of every other market: there is no single interest rate, and the curve is the cleanest synthesis of both the price of money (real rates) and its quantity. Yields drive the currency through rate differentials, and yields and currency together shape liquidity — the cost and availability of money that ultimately feeds back into growth, closing the loop. Markets are not moved by sentiment or euphoria; sentiment moves a single low-float name, but only liquidity and credit move the whole market. The chain is not symmetric. Growth, demand, and breadth panels are mostly confirmation — they tell you the cycle is intact. The catalyst for a regime change almost always sits downstream, in the yields and liquidity panels.

The Warsh Playbook
Why the curve flattened into the new Fed
“Financial market prices are probably the most important source of information to guide central bankers. When all the financial markets are doing is reflecting back what we’ve said, then we’re taking the most important source of information and being blind to it.”
Capital Flows Research · June 17th, 2026
The New Information Architecture
The Thesis

Kevin Warsh did not arrive to play dove or hawk. He changed what the Fed is for. The statement was shorter, forward guidance was dropped, and he declined to submit a dot. The intent is explicit: stop letting the Fed act as a filter on incoming data, and let market prices do the pricing.

The Market Already Ranks Data

His “better data” remark is not a plan to redefine CPI. It is an epistemic claim: the market already grades data quality. The 2-year jumps on CPI and barely twitches on University of Michigan. That asymmetry is the signal Warsh wants to lean on — the price is the verdict on which series carries information.

A Volatility Regime, Not a Narrative

Remove the Fed as a filter and you change the volatility regime, not just the talking points. MOVE sits near 71, compressed off its 115 high. The paradox: pulling forward guidance hands the clearing role back to data, so vol becomes data-driven rather than guidance-driven — quiet now, more reactive later.

2s10s, New Low

The market’s answer to the new regime is a curve that flattened hard. 2s10s printed roughly 30bp today — a fresh low for the cycle, down from 39bp yesterday and a 73bp peak. The new low is unmistakable: the curve is delivering the verdict the Fed refused to give.

What It Means

Twos repricing while tens fall is a short-end inflation-risk event, not a term-premium event. The long end is telling you two things: energy has not transmitted into core, and the productivity build-out (AI capex) caps the back end. If the Fed were behind the curve, this would be flipped — tens higher, twos lagging. It is the opposite.

So the flattening is not a recession signal here. It is the market pricing near-term inflation risk into the front while expressing confidence the back end stays anchored.

Pricing Hikes Is the Tightening
The Z6 Contract

The press conference repriced the forward curve hawkish. The Dec-2026 SOFR contract’s implied rate jumped ~15bp on the day to ~4.04%, up from a 3.04% low this winter. The contract has walked from pricing a pause toward roughly 40–50bp of hikes by year-end.

Path Dependency

The subtle point: the act of pricing hikes is itself the tightening. Financial conditions move on the contract, not on the funds rate. Warsh confirmed the mechanism without saying it — there was “one proposal on the table,” no discussion of cuts, and the committee was “unanimous and unambiguous.”

No forward guidance does not mean no signal. It means the signal lives in the strip, and the strip did the work this afternoon.

The Ceiling

Base case: not more than ~50bp of hikes this year, and the pauses come first. Oil is elevated but has not transmitted into core CPI, so the inflation impulse driving the front end is narrow, not broad.

Trade the contract, not the headline. Watch the z6 level for signs it is done repricing — that is where the next leg in rates and risk turns.

The Playbook
What Changed

Warsh removed the Fed as a filter: shorter statement, no forward guidance, no chair dot. The market becomes the instrument that prices data, and “better data” means data the market will actually respond to. This is an information-architecture change, not a dove/hawk pivot.

The Read

The flatter curve is the verdict: short-end inflation risk priced into the front, back end anchored by no energy pass-through and the AI productivity build. The z6 strip did the tightening by pricing ~40–50bp of hikes. Base case is resilience — stay constructive on risk, and watch bonds for the bottom that sets the bottom in equities.

The Risk

The line in the sand is the z6 contract. If the strip pushes toward 75bp+ of hikes or a higher terminal, the flattening becomes a tightening scare — wait for the flush before adding. Below that, treat this week’s repricing as the extreme and fade it.

Wide lens, narrow remit. Trade the strip, respect the curve, and let the price be the guidance the Fed no longer gives.

The Devaluation of the Yen & Carry Trade
A reflexive liquidity framework for the yen, JGBs & global risk
“The classic driver of USDJPY — the nominal US–Japan rate differential — decoupled from the currency around April 2025. The yen is no longer priced off the nominal rate the BoJ sets. It is priced off the real rate, a structural balance-of-payments shift, and a stretched carry position that periodically snaps.”
Capital Flows Research · June 18th, 2026 · Built from the daily livestream & research note
The Decoupling — Measured, Not Assumed
The Thesis

USDJPY used to be a rate-differential trade: where the BoJ set the nominal rate told you where the yen sat. Around April 2025 that relationship broke. The yen now prices off the real rate — held deeply negative because subsidy-driven fiscal policy suppresses measured CPI while the nominal rate creeps up — off a structural balance-of-payments shift, and off a stretched carry position.

The same fiscal impulse that holds CPI down funds the strategic-investment and defense build-out, circulates liquidity, and supports risk. Yen weakness, equity strength and offshore recycling reinforce each other until positioning gets extreme enough that a catalyst forces the rate-differential relationship to violently reassert.

The Measured Claim

We do not assume the decoupling, we measure it. Fit a fair-value model of USDJPY on the 2-year and 10-year differentials using only the pre-break sample, and the residual is the argument. The live 2y+10y fit puts fair value near 140 against spot ~160 — a ~20-figure gap; CapitalFlows’ published fit is wider still (~119, a 42-figure residual). Either way the gap is the fiscal-dominance, balance-of-payments and positioning story as a single number.

Decompose First

A USDJPY move is uninformative until it is split into a broad-dollar leg and a yen-specific leg. The tell is in the divergence. DXY sits near 100, well off its highs, while USDJPY presses fresh cycle highs near 161. The dollar is not making new strength against the world — the yen is making new weakness against the dollar.

The Hormuz energy shock killed the early-2026 dollar-bear narrative and re-anchored the dollar leg, re-widening nominal spreads even as they fail to work on the yen the old way. Markets now price a meaningful chance of a Fed hike by October. The dollar is loud — but the yen is still the louder of the two.

Negative Real Rates Are the Engine
You Are Paid to Borrow

The yen is priced off the real rate, and the 1-year real rate sat between −1% and −3% for years. It is still negative — but with core inflation cooling toward target and the BoJ at ~1%, it has lifted to roughly −0.6%, its least-negative in years. While negative you are still paid to borrow, so the carry keeps financing itself — but the move toward zero is exactly the anchor the trade has lacked, and the first crack the framework watches for.

Japan 1Y Real Rate (%)
Negative, but lifting toward zero
Inflation: Not Far Enough

Demographics held Japan in deflation for decades, which justified zero rates and yield-curve control. That broke in 2022; wages have run above 5% for three straight years and the BoJ ended the regime in March 2024, hiking to 1%.

But the contributions show a narrow impulse, capped at the headline by energy and subsidy components. Inflation is elevated yet cooling — enough to justify hiking, never enough to make the real rate positive. So the engine keeps running.

The Curve Tracks Inflation

Inflation drives the long end too. The 10s30s JGB spread moves in lockstep with core CPI — it steepened to ~150bp as inflation rose into 2025, and has since compressed ~40bp to ~110bp as CPI cooled. That long-end move is fiscal stress, not real-rate stress — the Ministry of Finance funds itself at the front of the curve. The compression back toward prior lows is the first real crack in the regime — and it has started.

The Risk Curve — Banks Are the Liquidity Gauge
Money in the System

When small-bank stocks rally to all-time highs, there is a lot of money in the system. TOPIX Banks has nearly doubled off its 2025 low to a record near 710 — the cleanest read on the liquidity Japan exports while the real rate stays negative and the MoF keeps issuing bills. The index is reflexive: real yields tick up, banks pull back, then melt up. The day this reverses, it shows up here first.

TOPIX Banks

New Lows & Short Vol

Every BoJ attempt to set a top through intervention has been rallied straight back through — the currency keeps falling while the BoJ raises rates and the MoF floods the system. Being long this carry is functionally being short volatility: implied vol is suppressed while skew stays elevated, the signature of a one-sided, complacent position. Every new low is just more liquidity added, until the flow reverses and the crowded trade unwinds violently.

The Distraction

The mechanism in one chart: the 10-year JGB yield keeps grinding higher — now near 2.6% — which looks like tightening, while the 1-year real rate stays below zero. The long end rising is a distraction — it does not bite, because the front-end real rate is negative and the MoF funds at the bills. As long as the real rate stays below zero, the yen falls and liquidity exports offshore — but watch it close on zero.

When It Reverses: August 2024

On 31 July 2024 the BoJ surprise-hiked to 0.25% and announced a JGB taper; days later a weak US payrolls print tripped the Sahm Rule. The anticipated collapse of the rate differential forced a synchronized unwind: short-covering sent the yen up >10% in days, margin calls hit, and the Nikkei 225 had its largest single-day drop since the 1987 crash. Japanese policy transmitted straight into world markets — the template for what the compressed spring releases.

The Tell — When to Be Long the Yen

The Structural Chain — Why Japan Funds the World
Japan is a structural carry machine: demographics force the cheapest money on earth, and the carry trade is the belt that wires it into global risk. It starts with a hard demographic fact — a population of ~122M shrinking ~0.5%/yr, a fertility rate of 1.15, and deaths outnumbering births by ~900k a year. A contracting workforce supporting a swelling 65+ cohort produces chronic weak demand and a savings glut that must be exported. Each step below forces the next — Japan did not choose to fund the world’s carry trade, its structure did. A demographic fact → the cheapest money on earth → a global carry trade → a volatility spike.

The Evidence — Three Papers
1 · Japan Structural
“What about Japan?” — Chien, Cole & Lustig, NBER WP 31850 (2023)

The consolidated state — Treasury + central bank + the GPIF pension fund — borrows cheaply and short, invests long and risky, and pockets the spread: a levered carry worth >6% of GDP. That is why net debt is just 78% of GDP, not the 230–270% gross headline. It holds because the debt is funded at home — the BoJ owns ~50% of JGBs and foreigners only 12%, insulating the state from foreign flight even as FY2026 debt service hits a record ¥31.3tn. The state already runs a carry trade.

2 · Carry Mechanics
“Carry Trades and Currency Crashes” — Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen, NBER Macro Annual 2008

Borrowing low-yield to buy high-yield beats the textbook — it violates uncovered interest parity — but the payoff is steady small gains then rare violent crashes. Carry returns are negatively skewed, subject to crash risk when funding liquidity and risk appetite decline. Picking up pennies in front of a steamroller.

3 · The Bridge
“Sizing up carry trades in BIS statistics” — McGuire & von Peter, BIS Quarterly Review (Sep 2024)

The yen carry is large, leveraged and largely off-balance-sheet, so it barely shows up in conventional debt statistics. Triangulating BIS data — the Triennial Survey and Locational Banking Statistics — puts $14.2tn of yen on one side of FX derivatives, and roughly ~$250bn of speculative carry going into the August-2024 unwind.

Carry Mechanics — What the Trade Actually Is
Borrow, Convert, Invest

1 · Borrow yen at 0.10–1.00% — the cheapest funding available.
2 · Convert — sell yen, buy dollars.
3 · Invest — hold USD at 4.5–5.5%, pocket the difference.

That is roughly +3.5% carry per year — but only if USDJPY stays flat. A weaker yen adds to the gains, which is why capital piles in and the loop reinforces itself. It began with Japanese retail (“Mrs. Watanabe”) reaching for yield, then was institutionalised at scale by hedge funds, banks and corporates.

Why Carry Pays at All

Theory (uncovered interest parity) says the high-yield currency should fall by exactly the rate gap, cancelling your pickup — predicted profit ≈ 0. Reality: the funding currency stays weak, so you keep most of the spread. That gap between theory and reality — the forward-premium puzzle — is the whole trade, and the payment is a risk premium.

Why the Yen Specifically

A wide policy-rate gap meets a currency that strengthens precisely when risk turns. Cheap to borrow in calm markets, but a safe haven that snaps back in stress — the currency you are short rallies just as your assets fall. The perfect trap, and the reason carry returns carry a fat left tail.

Global Plumbing — The Identity That Makes It Inevitable
A Surplus Is a Capital Export

Balance of payments is an identity: a current-account surplus is, by definition, a capital export. With goods trade near parity, Japan’s surplus is now driven by primary income (~¥42tn) — yields on past investments — a shift from an export economy to a rentier earning foreign returns. Excess national savings must flow abroad — that outflow is the yen that funds the world’s carry trade.

Sizing the Yen Carry

The trade physically moves capital across borders, levered and mostly hidden in derivatives. Per BIS data: $14.2tn of yen-side FX-derivative notional, ~$880bn of yen claims abroad, and ~$250bn of estimated speculative carry — most of it invisible in conventional debt data. Broader institutional surveys put the total yen capital scale near ¥506.6tn (~$3.2tn).

The stock behind it is Japan’s ¥562tn net foreign-asset pile, run by lifers, banks and the GPIF — the yen’s launch pad.

The Impossible Trinity

Fixed exchange rate, free capital flows, independent monetary policy — pick two. Japan keeps open capital and its own interest rates, and lets the yen float. That floating yen, sitting next to a huge rate gap, is the carry fuel. Intervention only buys time; in real trade-weighted terms the yen is its weakest since the early 1970s.

The Playbook
What Is Happening

The yen has decoupled from nominal rate differentials and now prices off the negative real rate, a structural balance-of-payments outflow, and a stretched carry. Fiscal dominance suppresses CPI, keeps the real rate negative, exports liquidity, and lifts the Nikkei and TOPIX Banks. Spot at 161 versus a 119 fair value is that story in one number.

The Read

While the real rate stays below zero, stay with the regime: weak yen, strong Japanese banks and equities, liquidity exported into global risk. The carry keeps paying — steady small gains — and being long it is being short volatility. Respect the asymmetry: the average is positive, the tail is violent.

The Trigger to Fade

The line in the sand is the real rate and the bank tape. When the 1-year real rate lifts toward zero, the 10s30s JGB spread compresses to prior lows, the MoF issues light, or TOPIX Banks rolls over — the spring releases and a far stronger yen unwinds the carry. That confluence is the moment to be long the yen.

What is this?

The rate complex is the operating system of every other market. There is no single interest rate — fed funds, SOFR, the 2-year, 5-year, 10-year, and 30-year each price a different question and transmit to different parts of the equity complex, the economy, and FX. SOFR and fed funds price what the Fed will do. The 2-year prices the policy path with marginal duration. The 5-year is the belly hinge between policy and term premium. The 10-year and 30-year are pure duration.

What is this?

The market signal is used as a broad window. Signal uses EMAs as the main indicator. EMAs determine the trend of the market.

The breadth signal is used in identifying whether the stock universe is broad or narrow. Signal uses MMTH as the main indicator. MMTH is a long-term indicator of the quantity of market participation.

The buying signal is used to determine the execution timing. Signal uses both NCFD and NHNL as the main indicators. NCFD takes a look at the underlying short-term momentum of the market. We set the upper threshold at 75 and the lower threshold at 25. Using this, NCFD is "Hot" when it hits the upper threshold, "Warm" when it lives below the upper threshold, "Lukewarm" when it lives above the lower threshold, and "Cold" when it hits the lower threshold. NHNL provides valuable information regarding stocks making new highs and stocks making new lows that can be used in determining the quality of the move of the market, whether up or down. While it can show whether we are in a stretch conducive of buying or selling, not all periods are equal in the timeliness of buying or selling. Therefore, NHNL is used in a contrarian manner depending on if it moves too fast by either velocity or level, or if it shifts direction during the market's upturn or downturn, or both.

Using all three in conjunction allows us to put out a certain thesis to the market and have a higher precision on pointing out market opportunities.
